Why does it do that?

There are two chemicals in the dish. Call them A and B. Fresh A seeps in everywhere at a steady rate, the feed. B is washed out at a steady rate, the kill. And there is exactly one reaction: when a B meets two other Bs in a pool of A, it turns that A into a third B. So B makes more B, but only where B already is.

On its own that would make blobs of B grow until they ran out of food. The trick is that both chemicals spread, and A spreads twice as fast as B. A blob of B eats the A around it faster than it can creep outward, so it sits inside a ring of depleted A. Any neighbouring blob finds the food already gone and dies back. Short range: make more of me. Long range: keep your distance. That is the whole recipe.

∂A/∂t = ∇²A − AB² + f(1 − A)A spreads, gets eaten, is fed
∂B/∂t = ½∇²B + AB² − (k + f)BB spreads half as fast, grows, is washed out

Spots are blobs that found their spacing. Stripes are blobs that could grow along but not across. The dividing cells of the mitosis preset are blobs that outgrew their own supply and split in two. Nudge the feed and the kill and the same two rules give you coral, fingerprints, or worms.

The Atlas preset grows all of them at once. It takes the band of the map along the arc, straightens it out and stretches it over the dish, so that every cell reacts at its own feed and kill: the feed rises from one end of the dish to the other, and across the dish the kill runs from just below the arc to just above it. Seeds are scattered evenly and coloured by where they land, and each stretch of the dish grows the pattern that belongs there: spots in one corner, stripes and worms through the middle, holes along one edge, and nothing at all where the kill wins. Point at the dish and the dot on the map shows where you are.

Stir moves the dish instead of painting on it. Under the chemistry runs a second simulation that you never see: water, after Jos Stam's stable fluids of 1999, the method that made computer fluids fast enough to play with. A drag pushes the water under it, the water carries the chemicals along, and pressure keeps the water from piling up anywhere, so a push in one place turns into a swirl. The swirl dies down in a few seconds, and the chemistry keeps going the whole time: coral wraps into a spiral and its lineages mix, and spots stretch into streaks, then pinch back into spots. Hold Shift to stir without leaving the brush.

The colours are not part of the chemistry. Every bit of B remembers which stroke it descends from, and new B inherits the colour of whatever made it, so each stroke founds a lineage. Where two lineages meet, the colours mix a little; where one outgrows another, it inherits the ground.

The surface is a trick of the light as well. The renderer reads the concentration as a low hill, works out which way each point of it faces, and lights it; the Made of row only changes what the hill is made of. Black Opal has smooth polished domes, a charcoal body, and irregular patches of colour beneath the surface that change with the light. Crystal Shell has a glossy purple outer layer with openings onto a green inner layer; the surface slowly shifts and reflects a soft studio light. Gold Ripple rolls warm metallic reflections over flowing ripples. Quicksilver reflects a small studio, wax lets the light out through its thin edges, neon lights nothing but the rim, and the watercolour pools its pigment where a real wash would. Lava, ice and Voronesque Gem add time: lava rolls red-orange turbulence over dark molten pockets with yellow-white hot ridges; Voronesque Gem shifts reflective rainbow cells over each curved blob; the frost glints twinkle and the cold mist drifts. Lava keeps its hot mineral colours, Voronesque Gem keeps its rainbow reflections, Gold Ripple keeps its warm gold, Crystal Shell keeps its purple and green, and Black Opal keeps its own colours; the other materials follow the selected palette. Contour draws the field's own level lines, the lines a weather map draws for pressure, and they keep going faintly outside each blob because the chemical does. The iridescent film gets its colours the way a soap bubble does, from light reflected off its top and bottom surfaces interfering, and the prism bends blue more than red, which is why its graph paper fringes. The chemistry underneath is the same.

Heart swaps the chemistry for the simplest model of excitable tissue, Barkley's. A cell fires when its neighbours push it over a threshold, and then it has to rest before it can fire again: one rule, plus the usual spreading. A pacemaker firing that rule in one spot gives the rings of a normal rhythm. Break a wave, with scar tissue or with a second stimulus landing in the refractory wake of the first, and the free end curls into a spiral that never has to stop, because it is forever chasing tissue that has just recovered. That spiral is re-entry, the mechanism of a flutter; several of them at once is fibrillation. A shock fires every cell at the same instant, so every cell is resting at the same instant, and the spirals have nothing left to run on. That is what a defibrillator does. The trace beside the sliders is the whole dish's voltage over time.

Quantum replaces the chemistry with the Schrödinger equation, the other great diffusion equation, the one with an i in it. The wavefunction is a complex number at every point: its size squared is the chance of finding the particle there, drawn as the hill, and its phase is drawn as colour. Throw a packet and it carries a wavelength, shorter the faster it goes: those are the rainbow stripes inside it. Watch them: the stripes drift backwards through the packet as it travels, because the phase moves at half the speed the packet does. Leave a packet alone and it spreads, since a particle with a definite place has no definite speed. Send one at two slits and the two halves interfere with each other on the far side, which is the experiment that makes the whole subject unavoidable; the trace beside the sliders is what a detector along the right edge would record. Send one at a thin wall taller than its energy and part of it comes through anyway. And put one in a bowl and it orbits without spreading at all, the closest a quantum thing comes to behaving like a ball.

The Schrödinger equation also runs backwards. Nothing in it forgets, so turn time around and everything retraces its steps, and the scheme that steps the dish forwards can be run backwards step for step. Press Rewind and the dish does just that: the fringes beyond the double slit gather back through the slits into the one packet that made them. Only what the absorbing edges swallowed is gone for good. The garden and the heart can't do this, because spreading smooths away the very details that running backwards would need.

Mould swaps the physics for a creature. Physarum polycephalum, the slime mould, is a single cell that can grow bigger than your hand, and it forages by sending out veins. It has no brain and no eyes, and in 2010 Nakagaki's group put oat flakes on a map of the Tokyo area where the towns are, set a slime mould down on Tokyo station, and watched it grow a network that matched the real railway for cost, efficiency and resilience. The dish runs Jeff Jones's model of it from the same year: thousands of agents, each of which looks ahead at three points, turns toward the strongest trail it finds, steps, and lays trail where it lands, while every step the trail blurs a little and fades a little. That is the garden's recipe turned inside out. Short range: go where the others went. Long range: forget. Veins are the paths enough agents agreed on to keep from fading, and the net coarsens because a vein that loses traffic loses its trail. Food leaks a scent that spreads farther and fades slower, which is how the mould finds it from a distance, and an agent that feeds takes the food's colour, so every vein ends up wearing the colour of what it eats. Reach is how far ahead the agents look, which sets the size of the mesh; fade is how fast trail goes, which sets how much the mould remembers.

With sound on, the dish listens. Kicks plant seeds in the garden, pace the heart, throw particles, or drop food for the mould; every kick pulses the surface and every second one turns the colours a step. In the garden, snares clear a patch. Loudness sets the pace, the brightness of the sound picks the colour of new seeds, and bass and treble lean on the feed and kill.

Alan Turing proposed this mechanism in 1952, reaction plus diffusion, as a way for a ball of identical cells to decide which end becomes the head. This particular pair of equations is the Gray–Scott model, and the presets come from John Pearson's 1993 survey of its patterns. Real Turing systems have since turned up in zebrafish stripes and in the ridges on the roof of a mouse's mouth. The slime mould is Jeff Jones's 2010 model, and the Railway preset is after Tero and colleagues' Tokyo experiment of the same year. The stirring is Jos Stam's stable fluids.

Things to try:

  • Choose Mitosis, clear the dish, and paint one dot. It divides. Then they divide.
  • Paint a line in Fingerprint and watch it fold.
  • Drag the point on the map slowly along the arc. Everything interesting lives next to that curve, and just off it everything dies.
  • Switch presets mid-growth. The old pattern becomes the seed for the new one, and when none of it can live there, new seeds go in.
  • Choose Atlas and point at the dish to find where each pattern lives on the map. Then choose Coral and watch the whole atlas become coral.
  • Stir Spots in a slow circle. The spots smear into a swirl of streaks, then pinch back into spots along it.
  • Paint two colours side by side in Coral and see where the border settles. Then do it in Storm.
  • Seed from a photo, then switch presets and watch the picture decide what to become.
  • In Heart, start with Sinus and paint a stroke just behind a ring, in the dark band where the tissue is still resting. Most of your stroke dies; the part that caught live tissue curls.
  • In Heart, turn the pace up past the tissue's recovery and watch every second beat get blocked. Then press Defibrillate during Fibrillation and watch the dish go quiet.
  • In Quantum, choose Free and tap once without dragging. Then throw one hard across the dish and watch its stripes run backwards.
  • In Quantum, raise the barrier slider during Tunnel and watch the part that gets through shrink. It never quite reaches nothing.
  • In Quantum, let Double slit run until the fringes form, then press Rewind. Try it in Box after a long wait: nothing there is lost, so it all comes back.
  • In Mould, choose Forage, pick a colour, and paint one dot of food far from the colony. Wait for a vein to reach it, then watch the vein turn that colour from the food back.
  • In Mould, paint a wall across the dish with one gap in it and food on the far side. The mould finds the gap, and the veins that led nowhere fade. In 2000 Nakagaki's group showed the real thing finds the shortest way through a maze.
  • In Mould, seed from a photo. The network grows into the picture and takes its colours.
  • On a phone, tilt it: the light moves.
  • In Look, choose Stage and drag the red lamp right up beside a blob. Its shadow is the colour of the other two lamps' light.
  • Choose Sun, then Fingerprint, and watch the shadows swing round and stretch as the sun sinks.
  • Choose Lamp with Glass, Prism or Wax and move the lamp low. Light goes through the domes, so each one's shadow glows in its own colour, brightest down the middle where the dome gathers it like a lens.
  • Play a beat, press Record, and stir. You have a music video.

On a computer the keys work too: Space pauses, F is full screen, C clears, E erases, S stirs, R rewinds, N scatters, P saves a picture, V records and L changes the light; 1 to 9 pick a preset, and G, H, Q and M pick the dish.
